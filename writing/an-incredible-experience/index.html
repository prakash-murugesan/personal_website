An incredible experience

Yes, I almost died. But I overcame it. Over and over again.

Remember, you learn more from a rough, seemingly insurmountable road than from a smooth, open one. This is a story of exactly that.

Here’s my trip log.

Wednesday

The red-eye to Panama. Panama to Lima. Lima to Cusco. And Cusco to Ollantaytambo.

Damn, what was I thinking? That is an insane trip. And somewhere along the way, pneumonia enters the picture. Shhh… foreshadowing.

I finally get to Ollantaytambo at night, and the room has bugs. A cockroach and some other things. But it’s too late. I need a shower, and I need to sleep.

I choose the bed where I didn’t see anything. Doesn’t mean nothing’s on it. I close the shades and finally get some good sleep after 24 hours of travel.

Thursday

Let’s explore Ollantaytambo. Somehow, I’m in a corner of town where there isn’t much around, so I have to walk into the center. I go to the ruins. Damn. How beautiful. I get a guide and explore the whole thing, climbing up to the top and back down.

I can feel that the air is… different. I need more of it somehow.

There’s an option to take another 30-minute walk to the highest point, at 10,000 feet. Why not? A short warm-up for tomorrow’s hike.

Damn, though. I feel it.

Back down, a long wait for lunch—a chicken sandwich—and some people-watching from the balcony. Then back to the room. A bit of a walk.

The dust makes me want to shower again. I do, but there’s no hot water in the new room they’ve given me. Whatever. I need the shower anyway.

I finish, come out, and get the chills. So I put on my wool base layer and get under three layers of blankets to shake it off.

By evening, I’m tired, but I need dinner. I walk back into town looking for pizza. A longish walk, but the place on Maps has shut down. I go to another one. It isn’t open when it’s supposed to be.

So I walk farther, to the back of town, outside the tourist zone, and get myself a pizza. 8 Llamas. Pretty good. I don’t choose the alpaca meat. Got a hike tomorrow. Not the time to be adventurous.

Back to the room. Dusty again. Another shower to wash it off. It’ll be my last chance for the next four days.

Pass out.

Friday

Hike day 1.

I wake up at 5. Pickup is at 6. I check my ring, and it says, “Major signs of strain.” Okay, my temperature is up by 0.8°F. Whatever. Must be the altitude. I’ll get used to it. I’m taking acetazolamide, or Diamox, to help with altitude sickness.

I meet my guide, Rody! Wonderful guy, full of life and excitement. We go pick up a Dutch couple. Dutch people are awesome! They’re really friendly. Tall and fit, too, so I’m like, damn, I gotta keep up.

We get breakfast and talk about the EU, politics, languages, and culture. He’s a funny guy, and his girlfriend is nice and warm. This is going to be fun!

We finish breakfast and drive to KM 82, where the hike starts.

I’m prepped, packed, glasses on, and ready to go.

We start moving. It’s mostly flat, with lots of little stops. Hmm. This isn’t so bad!

We reach the first climb. It isn’t steep, just a slight slope. Damn, I’ve packed my bag too heavy.

As we go, I can tell this isn’t easy. Not because of my legs. They don’t feel anything. For them, it’s a breeze.

But my lungs? There’s no breeze. I’m breathing, but it’s not enough. Huh.

Push, push, push, and we get to lunch. It’s a delicious spread. We eat and get 25 minutes to rest. Frank and Judith, the couple, fall asleep leaning on each other. I sit and try to make conversation with the other group, full of Germans. It’s pretty dry. Lol.

I touch my neck and head, and I feel… warm. Almost like a fever is coming. Nah. Must be the early wake-up. I’m probably just a little tired and have exerted myself climbing. It’ll get better.

After lunch, the climb is real. It’s up. We head to Wilkarakay, another Inca ruin. It’s a steep climb to get there. I’m pushing, pushing, pushing. This would be an easy walk for me in the Bay Area. But here, I’m already in zone 4. 167 bpm. Damn.

We get to Wilkarakay and take a break. What a beautiful piece of history. I explore it thoroughly.

Alright, time to descend. It’s steep. We need to get to Huayllabamba, following the river. Then we make a right and head toward Dead Woman’s Pass. Our camp for the night is partway up. Four hours to go.

Two hours in, it’s fucking hard. My legs, my muscles, are completely fine. In fact, they’re like, yeah, let’s go! But my lungs and heart are running a mile a minute. Even when I stop to catch my breath, I don’t get it back.

I tell the group, “I’m going to make it to Machu Picchu one way or another!”

The next second, I trip and almost fall off the edge of the trail, down the mountainside.

“Just don’t die along the way!” my guide says.

The altitude really is doing me in. The last two hours are hard. It rains intermittently, so my rain jacket goes on and off. I use those moments as breaks to catch my breath. But it’s not happening.

Frank and Judith have been here for two or three weeks. They’re used to these heights. For them, it’s a normal workout. They’re not fighting the air.

One hour to go. The sun will be setting by then, and we have to get to camp. Rody offers to carry my backpack, but my ego won’t let him.

I take small breaks, but I keep moving. We get to camp! Well in time!

Did it!

FUCK YES!

I get to my tent and lie down to catch my breath. But again, I can’t. I put my head down flat and stare at the top of the tent. A drop of water hits my head. The tent is leaking from the rain.

I shudder. I’m cold, wet from sweat and rain. I need to change. Warm up.

Just twisting and turning in the tent gets my breathing going a mile a minute again. That shouldn’t be the case.

It’s almost immediately time for tea and dinner. I put on three layers—a base layer, fleece, and puffy jacket—and walk six steps up to the food tent. My breath is gone again. I have to sit down.

I’m cold. Shaking. The food is good again. Frank asks what my resting heart rate is. I check. It’s 135 bpm. Almost zone 2 cardio while sitting.

“That’s not good…”

Yeah. Everyone reassures me that a night’s sleep will help. I think so too. I’m not gonna quit.

I tell everyone in the tent, “I’m going to make it to Machu Picchu one way or another! Even if it takes me a fifth day, I’m going to get there.”

I go back to my tent. There’s an extra rain tarp to stop the leak. I get into my sleeping bag and try to sleep.

I can’t.

Now that I’m lying down, I’m coughing. I can feel some phlegm. Okay, shit.

All decisions tomorrow. Sleep first.

I don’t get any.

Saturday

Hike day 2.

I check my Oura ring. Sleeping heart rate: 122 bpm. A brisk walk’s pace. Oxygen saturation: 88%.

That’s not good.

Wait. Does that mean I actually have to turn back? Give up? No.

Let me talk to the guides. They’ll have an idea. Maybe I can take some fever meds and push through.

Rody thinks I might be able to do it. Frank has some meds! Yes! Great news.

“I got you, man,” Frank says. “Meds. I even plan to carry your backpack. We’ll get you over Dead Woman’s Pass.”

4,200 meters above sea level.

I go to Roy, the head guide. A stern man who keeps his team in check. I ask him, “What do you think?”

He’s heard from the other guides that I’ve been coughing all night. He tells me about the one time something similar happened to one of his hikers. By day 2, the hiker had developed a cough and concerning sounds in his chest. They had to bring him down on a stretcher.

Machu Picchu will always be there. Safety first. But ultimately, the call is mine.

I say, “Yeah, safety first. I have a wife and child waiting for me back home.”

He asks if I’m taking Diamox. I am. He takes that as reassurance. He says he’s never heard of anyone developing a life-threatening altitude illness while taking it.

I think it over and talk with Rody. What’s my path back down? I’ll have to go at least as far as Huayllabamba and get a horse from there. Then, farther down, I’ll need a motorcycle ride back to the start.

I think of Akshaya and Veera. Alright. Machu Picchu will have to wait. I’ll go back, get a hotel, rest, recover from this fever, and take the train and bus to Machu Picchu. Meet the team on the same fourth day they’re supposed to be there!

That’s a plan. I’ll still make it.

It’s a tough goodbye. Frank and Judith are sad, but glad I’ll still see them there. Hugs and waves.

Rody sends a porter to guide me back down. I take my backpack, and we start walking.

I’m still breathing hard when I move. Flat and downhill are okay if I slow down. But even a slight incline, and I can’t take a step without immediately breathing like I’m doing zone 4 cardio.

Wait. This is harder today. I haven’t recovered at all.

14 kilometers to go.

The porter isn’t like Rody. He doesn’t speak English, and he does this every day, so he’s fast. Way too fast.

He goes ahead and waits. I catch up, and he goes ahead again. I’m annoyed.

I explain that I have a fever and altitude sickness. He kindly offers to carry my bag. My ego says no.

Every few steps, I’m gasping for air and hacking up a BAD cough. I spit out phlegm, and it’s brownish-red. Huh. Must be a bacterial infection, I think.

The porter offers to carry my bag again. This time, I say okay.

My heart sinks at every slight incline. I don’t know how I’m going to do it. Somehow, I do. Very slowly. A few steps, a hundred gasps.

We get to Huayllabamba, where there are supposed to be horses.

“No horse,” the porter says.

I’m confused. I’m not sure why. I don’t have the breath in me to argue or ask. I assume there aren’t any. We move on.

We get to Wilkarakay. It’s a straight climb. Feels like a thousand Inca stairs to the top.

I can only manage one step before I have to stop and catch my breath. He gets to the top and looks down for me. I’m halfway up the hill, sitting down. He brings my backpack down and offers me some water. Then he goes back up.

Leaving me trying to get enough air into my lungs for the next step.

I sit down. I’m feeling sleepy. I just want to lie down and sleep. It’s getting darker.

A slightly jealous coworker had sent me off with a backhanded wish: “I hope you don’t pass out from altitude! Haha.”

No. No matter what, I’m not gonna pass out.

Revenge gets me going.

The stairs are never-ending, but I make it to the top. I lie down. Try to catch my breath. Time passes, but my breath doesn’t come back. Every few steps, I’m coughing up more and more phlegm.

We start moving down. Okay. Must be all downhill from here. We should be close to the place where we get the motorcycle.

We reach the next village. Signs for motos. We pass them.

Another house with a bike. We go in and ask. There’s some conversation in Spanish. I sit down.

“No moto,” the porter says.

“Why?”

He says there’s another one farther down.

Okay. I follow him. At least it’s downhill, but I can’t do this anymore. That’s it. Next time I catch up to him, I’m gonna sit down, and he can bring one up to me.

But we get to a place with a bike big enough to take us both. A modified one with three seats. It’s flat from here on out, but I can’t walk anymore. I pay 100 soles for the ride.

He drops us off just before the river crossing at the exit. Instinctively, I grab my backpack instead of letting the porter take it. I don’t know why.

We cross the bridge, and it’s uphill again to the entrance. Another half a kilometer. I carry my backpack, coughing and gasping, up the ramp.

Just a little more. Just a little more.

A bus meets us and picks us up. I sit down. Finally! I’m done.

I have data. I don’t tell Akshaya anything. I type my symptoms into ChatGPT.

The response, essentially: don’t go to your hotel. Go to the ER now. This could be life-threatening.

I use the translation option to tell them I need to go straight to the ER in Ollantaytambo. They say okay. We get to town. They stop to drop off the porter’s stuff.

“Two minutes! Please.”

Two minutes? Now?

We get near the clinic, but the way is blocked. We have to walk around. They look for it. I know where it is.

It’s up a flight of stairs.

You’ve got to be kidding me.

I get to the top and use Translate to tell the nurse what’s going on. She gets progressively more worried. She brings a pulse oximeter.

My oxygen saturation is 66%.

She hooks me up to an oxygen tank while I keep explaining what happened. She thinks I have pulmonary edema. Fluid has built up in my lungs, making it hard for oxygen to reach my blood.

The oxygen tank isn’t enough. I only get up to 75% with it on. They need to admit me. The doctor rushes in and takes me to the ICU area of the clinic. They put a full mask on me and crank the oxygen up to 8 liters per minute.

Holy fuck. I can finally breathe!

They bring an ultrasound machine and an ECG machine and take my measurements.

My heart rate is all over the place. Not stable.

The doctor says there’s fluid throughout my lungs, AND I have pneumonia.

Pneumonia too?

They try to put in an IV. It doesn’t work the first time. Two more tries before they find the right spot. I’m hooked up and start getting treatment for the pneumonia and pulmonary edema, along with oxygen through the mask.

I ask when I can be discharged. He looks shocked by the question.

“You didn’t come in with a common cold. You are in an extremely critical condition. Your lungs, your heart, hypoxia, potential kidney failure. Our first priority is to evaluate you and get you stable.”

Okay… am I really not going to make it to Machu Picchu, then?

Sunday

I’m lying in the hospital bed. I look out the window, and it’s beautiful.

I realize that during the hike, I was so focused on catching my breath that I never really looked up at these mountains. Mountains revered as gods.

Sitting here, I see why. They are magnificent. They truly inspire awe.

Constantly hooked up to oxygen, I have nothing but time to look around. It’s Ollantaytambo’s founding anniversary week. There are traditional dances, songs, and art outside. The staff let me take my oxygen tank and IV line over to the window to watch.

It’s nice of them. What beautiful people. What a beautiful culture.

Three times a day, I get ultrasound and ECG checkups. The doctors and nurses patiently type into Google Translate, and we go back and forth using that and ChatGPT’s voice transcription. GPT Live sucks. I’m impressed by the tech, but it has serious flaws as a product.

With each round of blood work and examinations, they tell me how serious my condition was and that I’m getting better. My kidneys are okay. My heart has stabilized. But they can still see pneumonia and fluid in my lungs on the ultrasound.

It doesn’t look like I’m joining my group at Machu Picchu on Monday. The doctor says he’s seen people come in with this condition and stay four or five days.

I hold out hope. Maybe I’ll be better by Tuesday. It’s my last chance. I have a flight out of Cusco that night. Cusco is hours away in the other direction, and higher up. But at least I’ll land in Lima that night. Sea level. That should help.

I ask Muse to find me a ticket to Machu Picchu. There’s just one available, for a 7 a.m. circuit through the city. Worth a shot as a backup. So I buy it.

Nothing else booked.

It’s a long journey. Two hours by train to Aguas Calientes, then a bus to the top. To make my flight from Cusco, I’d need to come back down by bus in time for the 1:37 p.m. train from Aguas Calientes to Ollantaytambo. Then a three-hour drive to Cusco, along mountain roads with landslides, for a flight around 7 p.m.

Highly improbable. But I have hope.

Monday

It’s day 4 of the Inca Trail. My group will have made it to the top, looking down at the ancient city.

The doctor comes in and asks how I’m feeling.

“Much better!”

I actually do feel much better. Over the last day, they’ve been tapering my oxygen. From 8 liters per minute to 5, then 3, then 2.

I’m hitting 85% without oxygen and 92–94% with it. The doctor says we can try going down to 1 liter per minute.

There’s a new doctor on call today. He comes over and asks, “Do you really want to go to Machu Picchu?”

“¡Sí!”

But only if it’s safe and he thinks I’m ready.

He does an ultrasound and an ECG. He says there’s still fluid affecting about a third of my lungs, though I’ve recovered significantly. Some pneumonia remains. He explains that my lungs still need time to heal.

That sounds like bad news. The hospital team says, not necessarily.

If I really want to go, they can lend me an oxygen tank to take with me. If I feel like I need it, I can put it on.

Wow. I didn’t know that was an option! But the trip there and back, with so many legs and an oxygen tank, sounds hectic. Can I do all that back-to-back with so little margin for error?

My nurse asks why I don’t move my flight from Cusco to Lima to a later time.

Huh. I forgot that was an option.

Muse checks for me. It’s a free change to a 10:30 p.m. flight that arrives in Lima at 11:45.

Muse has already booked a hotel right at the airport. Even created a membership account and saved me $124. So arriving that late is fine!

This may actually work.

But the hospital staff say I’m still recovering and won’t be discharged yet. I should keep getting oxygen, IV treatment, and nebulization until the last minute. So I should take the last train from Ollantaytambo to Aguas Calientes.

Okay. Time to book everything. I ask Muse to book a hotel near the station so I won’t have to walk far. I pack my bags and put a small oxygen tank in my hiking backpack. The nurse runs out into the rain to get me a tuk-tuk to the station. That’s sweet.

I stand by the door, looking outside. It feels like a big moment.

I step out, get in, and wave goodbye to everyone. I’m off.

The train leaves in 20 minutes. It’s a one-way road. Gridlocked. Nothing moves. Should I just walk? But my things are heavy. Oxygen tank included.

I hold out hope. We get there with four minutes left before the last train leaves at 9 p.m. The line is massive. I’m sure I’m going to miss it.

But at 9:01, I’m sitting in the carriage.

Made it.

I chat with the Japanese solo traveler next to me and the older Peruvian Chinese couple across from me. I’m so happy to be outside again. Talking to people again.

We get to Aguas Calientes at 11 p.m.

Turns out the station is backed up, so the train has to pull farther forward. It drops me off a full eight-minute walk from the hotel. Okay. Eight minutes isn’t so bad.

It’s eight minutes steep uphill. Carrying an oxygen tank and dragging a suitcase.

It takes me right back to the descent from the Inca Trail. Every third step, I have to stop and breathe. It’s late at night, and I’m trying not to get robbed either. So I make my way up and across as quickly as I can.

I get to the hotel and sit down, trying to catch my breath. Thankfully, there’s someone ahead of me checking in.

First time I’ve ever said that.

I catch my breath. The front desk checks me in and says my room is on the fifth floor.

No elevator.

Of course.

I ask for help with my bags and make my way up, step by step. I tip him with all the change I have.

I take my first shower in days, making sure the steam doesn’t make it harder to breathe. It’s 12:30 a.m. I have to be up at 5:30 to catch the 6 a.m. bus to Machu Picchu.

I lie down. I start having palpitations. My breathing gets ragged again.

Shit.

No nurses or hospital staff to help.

I put on the oxygen and breathe. It helps. I wonder if the room has filled with steam, so I open the windows and prop myself up with pillows. I can’t sleep lying flat. It’s too hard to breathe.

I sit with the oxygen until 1 a.m. It helps enough that I can take it off and fall asleep propped up.

I’m going to make it to Machu Picchu one way or another.

Tuesday

The hotel staff call me at 5:30, even though I didn’t ask. That’s nice of them, because I would have slept longer. They ask if I need help with my bags.

“Yes, in ten minutes, please.”

I pack and get ready to go down.

I don’t think I can carry the oxygen tank for the two or three hours I’ll be at Machu Picchu. It’s a risk, but I choose to leave it behind.

I take what I can and get to the bus station. I still need to buy tickets.

The line for the bus is the length of the whole town.

Okay, then. I’ll try buying them online. Safari’s translation doesn’t work, so I buy them using whatever Spanish I understand. The tickets are sent to my Gmail.

I tap Gmail. It doesn’t open. For some reason, my iPhone has chosen to offload the app, and I need to download it again.

The data doesn’t work.

I ask around, trying to find where I can get the tickets I’ve already bought. It’s 6:15 a.m. I’m late.

A guide sees me looking around and comes over to ask if I need a guide and some help. I say yes. She takes me to the bus ticket counter and talks to them in Spanish.

Turns out they don’t have my tickets in the system. Lord knows what happened there. I say it’s okay. I’ll buy them again.

We print the tickets and join the line. Then I realize my Machu Picchu entrance ticket is also in Gmail. Which chose the best possible time to jump off my phone.

She asks the people behind us to hold our place, and we run—walk fast, in my case—to the tourism ministry office in town.

I don’t have Gmail, but I have Muse. I ask it to pull up my purchase invoice.

It does. Lifesaver.

That’s enough for them to let me into the building and print my ticket.

Thank you, government of Peru.

We get back in line. I’m nervous. I’m so close, but I don’t know how many obstacles are left.

I get to the boarding entrance. They scan my ticket. It beeps, and they let me through. I sit down on the bus.

Catch my breath again. Take my medication for the pneumonia and pulmonary edema.

I look out the window as the bus winds up the most beautiful mountain road to Machu Picchu. Eighteen switchbacks to the top. I can feel the altitude rising again.

We get off the bus. One last check. The entrance ticket to Machu Picchu.

I hold out my paper. It beeps.

I’m through.

I walk toward the city and finally see it. The mountains, the river, the rainforest, the deep valleys, and the stones that built an impossible city.

It’s breathtaking.

I wander through the city with my guide for the next two or three hours. Walking slowly. Learning. Amazed.

At the end of the tour, I ask to sit down. I don’t want to leave yet. My guide says to take my time. She’ll meet me at the exit.

I sit, looking over the city, and let its history wash over me. I let my thoughts connect everything I’ve been through to get here.

I did it.

It isn’t what I imagined. But somehow, it’s turned out even better. Life’s full of twisty roads and turns. It’s never an easy path. You just have to find a way through and smile along the way.

On the way back

I talk to anyone and everyone who’ll hear my story.

On the bus, I sit next to a Bolivian guy who runs a tour group. Turns out something similar happened to him in La Paz. He says I’m the only other person he’s met who’s been through it. He was taken to the ICU and barely survived. Now he doesn’t go above 4,500 meters.

In line for the train, I meet a tour group of older women. One of them is from California. From San Mateo! From Hillsdale!

My neighborhood. Incredible.

On the train, I’m seated next to three coworkers traveling across Peru together. I applaud them. That’s a nice group of friends at work if they can take a trip like that together.

The train has a balcony, so I go stand there. If they’d let me, I’d stay there the whole time.

It feels nice. I can breathe.

I run into a group of 15 people traveling together. They’re all Tamil, so I join in and start talking to them. Of course, I tell my story. And explain why I’m lugging an oxygen tank around.

They’re sweet. They take a picture and my WhatsApp number so we can stay in touch.

I say goodbye to everyone, get off in Ollantaytambo, and take a tuk-tuk back to the clinic. I haven’t been officially discharged yet.

They check my oxygen saturation. 91%. Better, though my heart rate is still high. I’m back in Ollantaytambo, higher than Machu Picchu. I need to get to Lima, at sea level. But first, I have to get through Cusco, the highest point of them all.

The clinic books a car with their trusted driver. It’ll come with an oxygen tank so I can use it during the three-hour drive.

They do another ultrasound and ECG and tell me there’s still some fluid in my lungs.

Until the last minute, they keep me on oxygen at the clinic and give me another nebulization treatment.

They help with my luggage and have the tank ready in the car. I say goodbye to the nurses and doctors. Thank them profusely for their lifesaving care. Their support. Their kindness.

I use the oxygen the whole way to Cusco. It helps.

Rody has made every effort to check on me since we parted. His last chance to see me is at the airport, so he takes a local bus there to meet me at 10 p.m.

The last person I see from my trip through Peru.

I have nothing but the nicest things to say about the people here. They are genuine and kind. They show you the best of humanity.

It gives me perspective. Fills me with love from absolute strangers.

It makes me hopeful for the future. When you want something with all your heart, the world, the universe, will bend to it. Make memories, smiles, and friends along the way.

My daughter

It isn’t until I get down and look at my symptoms that I really come face-to-face with the possibility that I might not be here.

What would she do?

It’s her. Her smile when she sees me. The love she shows me, even through the phone. The sadness in her eyes when she sees me hooked up to the oxygen tank.

“Appa fall down.”

My sweet baby. My world is you.

I’m coming back to you.

My wife

I don’t tell her until I’m in the clinic. But after I’m admitted, I have her with me the whole way. Through every setback, obstacle, joy, and memory.

She’s my other half. My partner in life. My beating heart, my breathing lung. The love of my life.

I love her. I miss her. And I can’t wait to be back in her arms.

Holding each other. From this day to my last.
